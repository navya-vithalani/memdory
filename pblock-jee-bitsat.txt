body:
### Inert Pair Effect
As we descend Group 13, the reluctance of the outer valence $s$-electrons ($ns^2$) to participate in chemical bonding is called the **inert pair effect**.

- Cause: Poor shielding of nuclear charge by the intervening $d$ and $f$ electrons ($3d^{10}$, $4f^{14}5d^{10}$).
- Stability trend:
  - $+3$ oxidation state: $\text{B} > \text{Al} > \text{Ga} > \text{In} > \text{Tl}$
  - $+1$ oxidation state: $\text{Tl} > \text{In} > \text{Ga} > \text{Al} > \text{B}$
- Consequences:
  - $\text{Tl}^{3+}$ acts as a powerful **oxidizing agent**: $\text{Tl}^{3+} + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Tl}^+$ ($E^\circ = +1.26\text{ V}$).
  - $\text{B}$ and $\text{Al}$ show exclusively $+3$ oxidation state in stable compounds.

NOTE: Lewis Acidity of Boron Trihalides
type: trick
color: sun
star: true
tags: grp13, back-bonding, lewis-acids, jee-advanced
body:
**Expected Order:** $\text{BF}_3 > \text{BCl}_3 > \text{BBr}_3 > \text{BI}_3$ (based on electronegativity of halogens).

**Actual Order:** $\text{BF}_3 < \text{BCl}_3 < \text{BBr}_3 < \text{BI}_3$

**Why?** Strong $2p\pi-2p\pi$ back-bonding occurs between filled $2p$ orbital of Fluorine and empty $2p$ orbital of Boron because of optimal size match. In $\text{BCl}_3$, $\text{BBr}_3$, and $\text{BI}_3$, back-bonding weakens drastically due to poor overlap ($2p-3p$, $2p-4p$, $2p-5p$).

Q: Why is the atomic radius of Gallium (135 pm) smaller than that of Aluminum (143 pm)?
A: Due to the presence of $10$ intervening $3d$ electrons in Gallium, which shield the nuclear charge very poorly, resulting in an increased effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$) that pulls valence electrons closer.
WHY: Aluminum has an inner configuration of neon ($[Ne]$) with only $s$ and $p$ electrons (high shielding). Gallium has $[Ar]3d^{10}$, and $d$-orbitals have diffuse shapes with weak screening.

WHY: $p\pi-p\pi$ back-donation is strongest in $BF_3$ due to matching $2p(\text{B})-2p(\text{F})$ orbitals, satisfying Boron's electron deficiency and reducing its Lewis acidity. In $BI_3$, $2p(\text{B})-5p(\text{I})$ overlap is ineffective.

body:
**Molecular Formula:** $\text{B}_2\text{H}_6$
**Structure:**
- 4 terminal $\text{B}-\text{H}_t$ bonds: Normal $2$-center-$2$-electron ($2c-2e$) covalent bonds.
- 2 bridging $\text{B}-\text{H}_b-\text{B}$ bonds: $3$-center-$2$-electron ($3c-2e$) banana bonds (tau bonds).
- Hybridization of Boron: $sp^3$
- Coplanarity: The 2 Boron atoms and 4 terminal Hydrogen atoms lie in one plane; 1 bridge H lies above the plane and 1 lies below.
- Bond angles: $\angle \text{H}_t-\text{B}-\text{H}_t \approx 120^\circ$, $\angle \text{B}-\text{H}_b-\text{B} \approx 97^\circ$.

@ Group 14 (Carbon Family) > Allotropes & Silicates

NOTE: Classification of Silicates
type: formula
color: sky
star: true
tags: grp14, silicates, inorganic-polymers
body:
Basic structural unit: $[\text{SiO}_4]^{4-}$ tetrahedron (Silicon $sp^3$ hybridized).

1. **Orthosilicates (Nesosilicates):** Zero shared corners. Formula: $[\text{SiO}_4]^{4-}$ (e.g., Willemite $\text{Zn}_2\text{SiO}_4$, Phenacite $\text{Be}_2\text{SiO}_4$).
2. **Pyrosilicates (Sorosilicates):** 1 shared oxygen corner. Formula: $[\text{Si}_2\text{O}_7]^{6-}$ (e.g., Thortveitite $\text{Sc}_2\text{Si}_2\text{O}_7$).
3. **Cyclic Silicates:** 2 shared oxygen corners per tetrahedron. General formula: $[(\text{SiO}_3)_n]^{2n-}$ (e.g., Beryl $\text{Be}_3\text{Al}_2\text{Si}_6\text{O}_{18}$).
4. **Chain Silicates (Pyroxenes):** 2 shared corners in linear polymer. Formula: $[(\text{SiO}_3)_n]^{2n-}$.
5. **Sheet / 2D Silicates (Phyllosilicates):** 3 shared oxygen corners. Formula: $[(\text{Si}_2\text{O}_5)_n]^{2n-}$ (e.g., Talc, Mica, Kaolin).
6. **3D Framework (Tectosilicates):** All 4 oxygen corners shared. Formula: $(\text{SiO}_2)_n$ (e.g., Quartz, Feldspar, Zeolites).

type: concept
color: indigo
star: true
tags: grp14, carbon-allotropes, fullerene, jee-advanced
body:
### Buckminsterfullerene ($C_{60}$)
- Soccer-ball shape (truncated icosahedron) with **32 faces**:
  - **20 six-membered rings (hexagons)**
  - **12 five-membered rings (pentagons)**
- Ring connectivity rule:
  - Six-membered rings are fused to both other hexagons and pentagons.
  - Five-membered rings are fused **only to six-membered rings** (isolated pentagon rule).
- Hybridization: Each Carbon atom is $sp^2$ hybridized with slight pyramidalization due to curvature.
- Two distinct $\text{C}-\text{C}$ bond lengths:
  - Single bond (hexagon-pentagon fusion): $143.5\text{ pm}$
  - Double bond (hexagon-hexagon fusion): $138.3\text{ pm}$

Q: Why does $CCl_4$ resist hydrolysis under normal conditions, whereas $SiCl_4$ hydrolyzes violently in water?
A: Silicon has accessible, vacant $3d$-orbitals to accept a lone pair from the water nucleophile ($H_2O$), forming an unstable 5- or 6-coordinated intermediate. Carbon has no low-lying $d$-orbitals in its valence shell.
WHY: Hydrolysis requires coordination of oxygen lone pairs prior to displacement of chloride. Carbon cannot expand its octet ($2s^2 2p^2$), whereas Silicon can ($[Ne]3s^2 3p^2 3d^0$).
TAGS: grp14, hydrolysis, coordination, jee-main

Q: Why does Lead(IV) iodide ($PbI_4$) not exist, whereas $PbCl_4$ and $PbF_4$ exist?
A: $\text{Pb}^{4+}$ is a strong oxidizing agent (due to inert pair effect) and $I^-$ is a powerful reducing agent; $Pb^{4+}$ immediately oxidizes $I^-$ to $I_2$, reducing itself to $PbI_2$.
WHY: $\text{Pb}^{4+} + 2I^- \rightarrow \text{Pb}^{2+} + I_2$. The bond energy of $\text{Pb}-\text{I}$ is also too low to overcome the inert pair ionization energy.

NOTE: Drago's Rule in P-block Hydrides
type: trick
color: sun
star: true
tags: grp15, chemical-bonding, dragos-rule, jee-advanced
body:
**Drago's Rule Condition:**
If the central atom belongs to period 3 or higher, has at least one lone pair, and the attached terminal atoms have electronegativity $\le 2.5$ (e.g., Hydrogen), **no hybridization occurs**.

- In $\text{PH}_3$, $\text{AsH}_3$, and $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, the bond angle is $\approx 90^\circ-93^\circ$.
- Bonds are formed by pure, unhybridized $p$-orbitals.
- The lone pair resides in an almost pure $s$-orbital with high $s$-character, making it tightly held and an extremely weak Lewis base!

Q: Why is Hypophosphorous acid ($H_3PO_2$) a stronger reducing agent than Orthophosphorous acid ($H_3PO_3$)?
A: Because $H_3PO_2$ possesses two directly bonded $P-H$ bonds, whereas $H_3PO_3$ has only one $P-H$ bond. Reducing ability in phosphorus oxoacids is directly determined by the number of $P-H$ bonds.
WHY: $P-H$ bonds have hydridic character ($H^{\delta-}$ due to similar electronegativities: P = 2.19, H = 2.20) and easily supply electrons/hydride to reduce metal ions like $Ag^+$ to metallic silver.

Q: What is the physical state and structure of Phosphorus Pentachloride ($PCl_5$) in the solid state?
A: In the solid state, it exists as an ionic solid consisting of tetrahedral $[PCl_4]^+$ cations and octahedral $[PCl_6]^-$ anions: $[PCl_4]^+[PCl_6]^-$.
WHY: In the gas phase, $PCl_5$ is a monomeric trigonal bipyramidal molecule ($sp^3d$). In the crystalline solid, ionization optimizes lattice energy and electrostatic stability.

A: It readily dissociates into Phosphorus Trichloride ($PCl_3$) and Chlorine gas ($Cl_2$): $PCl_5 \xrightarrow{\Delta} PCl_3 + Cl_2$.
WHY: The two axial $P-Cl$ bonds are longer and weaker than the three equatorial bonds due to greater repulsions (each axial bond experiences 3 repulsions at $90^\circ$ versus 2 at $90^\circ$ for equatorial).

type: concept
color: indigo
star: false

body:
Oxygen differs from sulfur and higher members due to:
1. Small atomic radius and high electronegativity (3.44, second only to Fluorine).
2. High bond dissociation energy of $\text{O}=\text{O}$ double bond ($498\text{ kJ/mol}$).
3. Absence of vacant $d$-orbitals in valence shell $\rightarrow$ maximum covalency of 4 (practically limited to 2 or 3 in $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$).

Q: Why is ozone ($O_3$) a powerful oxidizing agent at room temperature?
A: Because it is thermodynamically unstable with respect to dioxygen and spontaneously decomposes to release highly reactive nascent oxygen: $O_3 \rightarrow O_2 + [O]$.
WHY: The decomposition of ozone has negative $\Delta H$ (exothermic) and positive $\Delta S$ (increase in moles of gas), making $\Delta G$ very negative ($\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S < 0$).

@ Group 17 (Halogens) > Interhalogens & Acidity

NOTE: Acidic Strength of Chlorine Oxoacids
type: concept
color: indigo
star: true
tags: grp17, oxoacids, acid-strength, high-yield
body:
**Order of Acidity:**
$$\text{HClO} < \text{HClO}_2 < \text{HClO}_3 < \text{HClO}_4$$
**Mechanism:**
- As the oxidation state of Chlorine increases ($+1 \rightarrow +3 \rightarrow +5 \rightarrow +7$), the number of terminal electronegative oxygen atoms ($=\text{O}$) increases.
- Conjugate base stability:
  - $\text{ClO}^-$: 0 equivalent resonance structures (charge localized).
  - $\text{ClO}_2^-$: 2 equivalent resonance structures.
  - $\text{ClO}_3^-$: 3 equivalent resonance structures.
  - $\text{ClO}_4^-$: 4 equivalent resonance structures delocalizing negative charge over 4 oxygens.
- Greater resonance stabilization of the conjugate base anion leads to stronger acid!
---

NOTE: Electron Gain Enthalpy Anomaly
type: trick
color: sun
star: true
tags: grp17, electron-affinity, exceptions, jee-main
body:
**Expected Order:** $\text{F} > \text{Cl} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$
**Actual Order:** $\text{Cl} > \text{F} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$

- $\Delta_{eg}H^\circ(\text{Cl}) = -349\text{ kJ/mol}$
- $\Delta_{eg}H^\circ(\text{F}) = -328\text{ kJ/mol}$

**Explanation:** Fluorine has an extremely compact $2p$ subshell. Adding an incoming electron experiences massive interelectronic repulsions from the already crowded 7 valence electrons. In Chlorine, the incoming electron enters the larger $3p$ orbital with significantly less repulsion!

Q: What is the molecular geometry, hybridization, and number of lone pairs in Chlorine Trifluoride ($ClF_3$)?
A: T-shaped geometry, $sp^3d$ hybridization, with 3 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs located at the equatorial positions.
WHY: Central Chlorine has 7 valence electrons + 3 from Fluorine = 10 electrons (5 pairs). VSEPR dictates that the 2 lone pairs occupy equatorial sites to minimize $90^\circ$ lone pair-bond pair repulsions.

Q: Why is Fluorine ($F_2$) a stronger oxidizing agent than Chlorine ($Cl_2$), despite Chlorine having a higher negative electron gain enthalpy?
A: Because of the exceptionally low bond dissociation enthalpy of the $F-F$ single bond and the extremely high hydration enthalpy of the small fluoride ion ($F^-$).
WHY: Standard electrode potential $E^\circ$ depends on $\Delta H_{\text{atomization}} + \Delta_{eg}H + \Delta H_{\text{hydration}}$. The weak $F-F$ bond (due to lone pair repulsion in compact $2p$) and huge hydration of $F^-$ overwhelm the electron gain enthalpy.

@ Group 18 (Noble Gases) > Xenon Compounds & Geometry

NOTE: Bartlett's First Synthesis of Noble Gas Compound
type: concept
color: indigo
star: true
tags: grp18, xenon, historical, discovery
body:
In 1962, Neil Bartlett noticed that platinum hexafluoride ($\text{PtF}_6$) oxidized molecular dioxygen ($\text{O}_2$) to form an ionic compound:
$$\text{O}_2 + \text{PtF}_6 \rightarrow \text{O}_2^+[\text{PtF}_6]^-$$
He realized that:
- First ionization enthalpy of molecular Oxygen: $1175\text{ kJ/mol}$
- First ionization enthalpy of Xenon: $1170\text{ kJ/mol}$
Since their ionization energies are almost identical, Bartlett reacted Xenon with $\text{PtF}_6$ gas at room temperature, successfully isolating the first noble gas compound: **$\text{Xe}^+[\text{PtF}_6]^-$ (orange-yellow solid)**!

NOTE: Complete vs Partial Hydrolysis of Xenon Fluorides
type: formula
color: sky
star: true
tags: grp18, xenon, hydrolysis, reactions, high-yield
body:
1. **$XeF_2$ Hydrolysis (Redox):**
$$2\text{XeF}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{Xe} + 4\text{HF} + \text{O}_2$$
2. **$XeF_4$ Hydrolysis (Disproportionation):**
$$6\text{XeF}_4 + 12\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{Xe} + 4\text{XeO}_3 + 24\text{HF} + 3\text{O}_2$$
3. **$XeF_6$ Complete Hydrolysis (Non-redox):**
$$\text{XeF}_6 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{XeO}_3 (\text{explosive solid}) + 6\text{HF}$$
4. **$XeF_6$ Partial Hydrolysis (Stepwise):**
$$\text{XeF}_6 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{XeOF}_4 (\text{colorless liquid}) + 2\text{HF}$$
$$\text{XeOF}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{XeO}_2\text{F}_2 + 2\text{HF}$$

Q: What is the molecular geometry and hybridization of Xenon Fluorides?
A: Xenon Difluoride ($XeF_2$) is linear in geometry with $sp^3d$ hybridization (2 bond pairs and 3 equatorial lone pairs).
WHY: 8 valence electrons + 2 from fluorine = 10 electrons (5 electron pairs). The 3 lone pairs reside in the equatorial plane at $120^\circ$ angles, canceling their dipole moments.

Q: What is the molecular geometry and hybridization of Xenon Fluorides?
A: Xenon Tetrafluoride ($XeF_4$) has a square planar shape with $sp^3d^2$ hybridization (4 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs situated opposite to each other in axial positions).
WHY: 8 valence electrons + 4 from fluorine = 12 electrons (6 electron pairs). The 2 lone pairs take trans positions to achieve minimal electronic repulsion ($180^\circ$).

Q: What is the molecular geometry and hybridization of Xenon Fluorides?
A: Xenon Hexafluoride ($XeF_6$) has a distorted octahedral (capped octahedral) shape in the gas phase with $sp^3d^3$ hybridization (6 bond pairs and 1 active lone pair).
WHY: 8 valence electrons + 6 from fluorine = 14 electrons (7 electron pairs). The presence of the sterically active lone pair distorts the regular octahedron.

Q: What are the products of complete hydrolysis of Xenon Hexafluoride ($XeF_6$)?
A: Xenon Trioxide ($XeO_3$) and Hydrofluoric acid ($HF$): $XeF_6 + 3H_2O \rightarrow XeO_3 + 6HF$.
WHY: This is a non-redox replacement reaction where the oxidation state of Xenon remains $+6$ throughout. $XeO_3$ is a colorless, dangerously explosive hygroscopic solid.
